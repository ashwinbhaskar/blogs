Inside an autoregressive LLM

Before we start

The title, taken apart

“LLM” is short for large language model, so the title holds three ideas: a language model, which is large, and which is autoregressive. We take them in the order a reader needs them.

Language model (the LM)

A program that takes some text and answers one question: what comes next? Not with a single guess, but with a probability for every possible continuation. The units it works in are tokens: a word, or a piece of one (slide shows how text is cut into them). The next slide shows a real answer from a real model.

Autoregressive

How such a program writes: one token at a time. Ask it for the next token, take a likely one, stick it on the end of the text, ask again. Every new token is therefore predicted from all the text so far, the program's own earlier picks included. Auto: its own; regressive: predicting from what came before.

Large (the first L)

A count of the adjustable numbers inside the program. What those numbers are, and where they come from, comes a few slides on, once we have seen the program run.

Before we start

A language model guesses the next token

Feed it text and it returns a probability for every possible next token. Above, GPT-2 small (2019), the example model for this whole series (small enough to run on a laptop, open enough to look inside), reads our nine-token sentence minus its last word. It bets on too, so, trying. (hungry is 13th, at 0.9%.)

Pick one of those, append it, run again: the loop you just watched, “autoregressive” in action. A chat reply is this loop run a few hundred times.

The picture draws the model as a box. The program inside the box is a transformer: a particular recipe, published in 2017, that nearly every language model now follows. This series opens that box; the next three slides are orientation first.

Before we start

Large: what is inside the box

Nobody writes the rules of a language model by hand. Inside the box is one long formula with an enormous number of adjustable numbers in it, its parameters; “large” counts them.

124 million

GPT-2 small (2019), the model in this series

7 billion

LLaMA-2 7B (2023), a typical “small” model today

Hundreds of billions and up

The largest models today

Training is where the numbers come from

The program reads a huge amount of text and, at every position, guesses the next token. After each guess, every parameter is nudged a little in the direction that would have made the guess better. Over billions of tokens the guesses become good: that is training. A formula tuned this way is called a neural network.

Mostly multiplication

Most parameters sit in rectangular grids of numbers, matrices, and most of the formula is: turn the text into numbers, multiply by a grid, repeat. So this series goes one matrix at a time: open each grid and ask what its multiplication is for.

Before we start

Where this kind of model sits in the family

“Looking” below: while the formula works on one token, which other tokens' numbers may it use?

Encoder-only

BERT (2018). An encoder is a network that reads: the whole text at once, every token looking at every other, before and after. Good at labelling and search; it does not write text.

Encoder-decoder

The original 2017 transformer. An encoder reads the input, then a decoder, a second network, writes the output. Built for translation.

Decoder-only

GPT, LLaMA, and the chat assistants built on them. One network reads and writes; each token looks only at itself and the tokens before it: the autoregressive kind. What “LLM” usually means today, and this series' subject.

All three are built from the same pieces; the difference is who may look at whom. The piece that does the looking is called attention, and it is what Part 1 opens.

The big picture

From text to numbers: tokens and embeddings

A tokenizer chops text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary (GPT-2's has 50,257 entries). Common words are one piece; rare ones are split: un|bel|iev|ably. Each piece is a token and gets an id: its position in the vocabulary.

Each id picks a row of an embedding table E: 768 numbers (in GPT-2 small), the token's vector. The rows start random; training adjusts them until tokens used in similar ways get similar rows. Stack our nine rows and add a little position information to each (the formula will not look at row numbers, so the order has to be written into the numbers). That is X: the numbers the formula works on.

The big picture

The whole transformer, zoomed out

Text goes in on the left, next-token probabilities come out on the right, and in between sits a stack of N layers, all of the same design but each with its own parameters: GPT-2 small stacks 12, LLaMA-2 7B stacks 32. Each layer is two blocks: attention, then a block called the MLP (short for multilayer perceptron, an old name for a plain neural network).

Part 1 takes the attention block, Part 2 the MLP block, Part 3 follows a token up through the layers, and Part 4 covers the LM head, the last step, which turns the last token's vector, as it comes out of the last layer, into probabilities. The small “norm” boxes rescale the numbers to keep them well behaved; they get their turn in Part 3.

The big picture

Zooming in: one layer, two blocks

Attention block: the tokens exchange information. It is the only place in the whole model where one token's vector can influence another's. (Part 1.)

MLP block: each token is transformed on its own, without looking at the others. (Part 2.)

Both blocks add their result onto X (the plus signs) instead of replacing it, so what reaches the next layer is the same X, corrected twice. Part 1 ends on the attention block's plus sign.

In one sentence: attention is the step where every token gathers information from itself and the tokens before it, weighted by how relevant each of them looks. Part 1 is that sentence, made precise.
